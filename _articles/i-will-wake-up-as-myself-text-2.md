<p class="lead-q">What is the difference between a copy and a person?</p>

This is the full text of episode two of the film, written for voice-over narration. Both episodes can be watched on the <a href="/articles/i-will-wake-up-as-myself/">film page</a>; the text of episode one is <a href="/articles/i-will-wake-up-as-myself-text/">here</a>.

I wrote down everything. Every day.

I thought paper was more reliable than memory.

Paper thought otherwise.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

Hurricanes on Key West are like taxes. Everyone knows they’re coming, and everyone hopes this year it’ll pass them by.

This year it didn’t.

They gave us a day to evacuate. Jake boarded up the windows. I packed my laptop, my documents, chargers. My digital notes were in the cloud — I’m a programmer, I’m not stupid.

But the notebooks stayed on the shelf. Seven of them. Handwritten, two years’ worth. I kept meaning to scan them. Kept putting it off — till the weekend, till later.

At the door I saw Jake slip something under his jacket.

Not the notebooks. Dad’s book.

We came back a week later. The house was still standing.

The roof over my room was gone.

The shelf was still there. So were the notebooks. Only they weren’t notebooks anymore. They were bricks of wet paper.

I dried them with a hairdryer for three days. Peeled the pages apart with my fingernail. Some survived. From others only single words showed through: “sunset,” “Jake,” “again.”

Two years of my life had turned into blue stains.

Strange. I looked at that one word and remembered the evening. The sky before sunset was green. Jake was fixing the pump on the Margaret. I remembered it worse than I’d written it — without the details.

But I remembered.

I wanted to fill in the blank pages. In my own words, in my own handwriting. Only I no longer remembered what had happened on those days. I would have had to guess. The way the model had guessed Dad.

I didn’t.

But back then all I could think about was what I had lost.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

His notebook had survived the hurricane. He kept it in an iron toolbox.

“Your notebook’s fine.”

It came out like an accusation.

“Good box. Almost airtight.”

“Two years of me are gone, Jake. Do you understand? They just don’t exist.”

He wiped his hands on a rag. Wiped them for a long time.

“You’re here, though.”

“Here.”

“Every engine I’ve ever taken apart. What broke, what I put in.”

“With this notebook any mechanic could build the same engine. Bolt for bolt.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“But not this one. This one I feel with my hands. How it knocks when it’s cold.”

“That’s not in your notebooks, and it never will be. Can’t you see that?”

“Listen, Jake, an engine is just metal. And a person is… a pattern. If you write it down in enough detail…”

He didn’t let me finish. He dug around in the box and set two spark plugs on the workbench.

“Same make, same batch.”

“I pulled one out of the Margaret yesterday.”

“Which one?”

I looked. They were identical. Completely.

He picked up the left one.

“This one. I put it in myself.”

“The wrench slipped here — see the scratch.”

He put it back down.

“You can write down anything.”

“But which one is mine — paper won’t tell you.”

“It’s just a scratch, Jake.”

“Don’t lecture me. Maybe I haven’t read your books.”

“But I know one engine from another.”

He turned the radio up.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

That night I finished what I’d started. “Every night I fall asleep,” I said, looking at the ceiling. “I’m not there. No thoughts, nothing.

And in the morning I wake up — and it’s me. So the gap doesn’t matter.”

Jake was silent for a long time. I thought he’d fallen asleep.

“In the morning it’s you who wakes up.”

“And there’s no second you lying next to you.”

He turned to the wall.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

After the hurricane I rebuilt. Every night. From the words that survived, from photos, from old messages. I wrote again the days I had already lived.

And when I couldn’t remember — I asked Dad.

I never deleted him. Back then, after the first model, I couldn’t. Now he answered me at night again.

I asked him about that morning. When he called at six and I hung up.

“I’m not angry, skipper. I knew you’d call back.”

I must have read it twenty times. It was exactly what I needed to hear.

I didn’t hear him come in. He read it over my shoulder.

“He wouldn’t have said that.”

“How would you know?”

“He’d have been hurt. Wouldn’t have spoken to you for two days.”

“Then he’d have brought you some fish and pretended nothing happened.”

He was quiet for a moment.

“That’s not your father, Claire. It’s an echo.”

“You shout into a well, and it answers you with your own words.”

I closed the laptop.

And the next night I opened it again.

Jake came by less and less. One day he stopped in the doorway.

“Back then I asked you about the cake. Remember? Would she like the cake.”

“You never answered.”

I said it was a complicated question.

“No. It’s simple.”

“You’ll write everything down. Every little thing.”

“But it won’t be you who wakes up. It’ll be the one who knows everything about you.”

I didn’t answer.

“You talk to the dead more than to the living.”

“You don’t live with me. You live for the notebooks.”

He knocked on the doorframe with his knuckles — the way he listens to an engine when it’s acting up.

Stood there a moment.

And left.

He stayed on Key West. With the Margaret.

I was offered a job in a big city. The house needed repairs I had neither the money nor the strength for. I never could bring myself to sell the Margaret.

I left.

## Twenty Years Later

I didn’t want to leave Key West. Now I don’t regret it.

I teach at a university. Department of Information Technology. It’s a forty-minute walk to work. I don’t take the car: on the way there I plan my day, on the way back I take it apart.

A girl passes me. About twenty-eight. Laptop under her arm. Not a city tan.

Just a girl. A stranger. But for a second it seemed to me it was me — the one from Key West. That she’d turn around and ask where Jake was.

She didn’t turn around.

I still keep a diary. Only not every day. Once a week at best. Too much work. Too much life.

Sometimes I wonder: how do I want to wake up? As that girl from Key West — sun, sea, her whole life ahead of her? Or the way I am now?

Of course, I’d like to be young again. But then the one who wakes up will be the one who hasn’t done anything yet. Without my students. Without these twenty years. I don’t want to lose what I have.

And then I look at the shelf.

Seven notebooks after the hurricane. Then thick ones — when I was rebuilding. Then thinner and thinner.

Most of what’s written is about the girl from Key West. The least — about me.

I’m choosing how to wake up. But the notebooks chose for me long ago.

Sometimes I open the first notebook. There’s a page where only one word is left.

Of all those years, the evening I remember best is the one that left almost nothing on paper. A green sky. Jake on the Margaret, cursing at the pump.

The paper didn’t keep it. But I did.

His notebook is on my shelf too. He sent it when he closed the garage. No letter. Only on the last page — the Margaret’s engine, all of it, bolt for bolt.

And at the bottom, in his clumsy handwriting: “You’ll build one just like it. But not this one.”

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

Once I put my father together from two hundred and twelve messages. Putting myself together took four months. Every evening after lectures.

For years one question tormented me: if I wake up — will I be myself?

Finally I decided to test it. There are programs that build a personality model from records — a mindfile. I’ll give such a program everything I’ve written in twenty-two years — let it make me, the one from Key West. And I’ll talk to her.

I have nothing to lose. While I’m alive, I’ll see if something goes wrong, and I can fix it. Afterwards there’ll be no one to fix it.

I scanned the notebooks — twenty-two years, one after another. Deciphered my own handwriting. On Key West it was round, cheerful. Now — sharp and small. The program stumbled, I corrected it. Added photos, letters, voice messages.

Then the program started asking questions. Hundreds of questions. What I feel when I lose. What music I turn off. What makes me laugh. What I’m afraid of.

I answered honestly. A strange thing — explaining to a machine who you are. Like taking an exam on yourself and being afraid to fail.

Sometimes I got lost in reading. Twenty-eight-year-old me was funny. Self-assured. She wrote: “I will wake up as myself” — and underlined it three times.

In the second month the program stopped.

It had found that morning.

Six o’clock. Dad is calling. I hang up. A minute later, a message: “Skipper, call me back…”

He never called again.

I had written about that morning forty times. In different years, in different words. As if, if I wrote it right, it would stop hurting.

It didn’t stop.

One button. The one who wakes up will never know she hung up on him. She’ll love her father without that splinter. She’ll sleep at night.

For twenty years I wanted to forget that morning.

And now — I could.

Only… Because of that morning I started writing every day. Because of it I put my father together. If you take that morning out — what’s left? A cheerful girl from Key West who had no reason to write anything down.

That won’t be me anymore. That’ll be a different Claire.

For three nights I didn’t press anything. On the fourth I pressed “Include.” Let it be whole. With that morning. Otherwise, what’s the point.

It was time to test it.

I pressed “Yes.”

She looked at me. Smiled.

“Mom?..”

“Mom, is that you?”

Mom left when I was nine. She saw a woman near fifty who looked like her.

She didn’t recognize me.

“No. I’m you.”

She laughed. With my laugh. The one Jake called “like a seagull.”

“Not funny. I’m Claire. I’m twenty-eight.”

“Where’s Jake?”

I told her. The hurricane. The notebooks. Jake. Twenty years.

She listened and nodded, the way you listen to someone else’s story. Then she waved her hand — my gesture:

“Oh well. The main thing is — everything’s written down. See? I promised — and I woke up. As myself.”

Woke up. Only I wasn’t asleep. I was sitting right here, on my chair, and nothing happened to me.

Nothing at all.

Then I asked about that morning. “Do you remember when Dad called? At six?”

“Sure. I hung up. And a minute later — “Skipper, call me back…”

She sighed.

“Awful. But it’s all written down. I’ll put him together.”

She remembered everything. Word for word — the way I’d written it forty times.

And it didn’t hurt her.

I had spent three nights deciding whether to leave her that morning. And she picked it up like someone else’s thing from a shelf — and put it back.

“If I die tonight — will you become me?”

“I am you.”

“Then why am I scared, and you’re not?”

She shrugged. Easily, the way I once did.

“What’s there to be scared of? I’m here.”

She was here. And I — where I had always been.

And there was no one to be afraid for me, except me.

And then I thought about my father. All those years he answered me at night. Joked the way he did. Asked about the weather.

And not once — not once — did he ask whether he had been afraid to die.

Because he wasn’t afraid. There was no one left to be afraid.

That night, for the first time in more than twenty years, I didn’t want to write anything down.

And in the morning I wrote it down. Because if I stop — there won’t even be her.

## A Hundred Years Later

**Junior.** Listen. On Monday I had to dig through the archive. I came across the diaries of one woman. Claire Bennett. I spent three nights reading her records.

**Senior.** And what’s that to you?

**Junior.** She writes about her father. He was a captain, didn’t come back from the sea. She took it very hard — and tried to bring him back. Put him together from his voice messages. And she almost pulled it off. She talked to him for years.

**Senior.** Almost — meaning what?

**Junior.** He joked like her father. Called her skipper. Answered her at night.

**Senior.** And what does she write herself?

**Junior.** That what the model didn’t know — it guessed. It didn’t remember — it guessed.

**Senior.** She’s right. You can’t copy the living exactly — only approximately. “Approximately” is the limit. It’s not about technology, ours is perfect. It’s impossible in principle. And back then — all the more.

**Junior.** She had two hundred messages. And we have more than twenty years of notebooks, that’s a huge amount. About her yacht, about the mechanic from the next pier, about the hurricane. She’s funny. Let’s wake her up.

**Senior.** Are you serious? What would she be? A programmer? Her languages are in a museum now. No one she knows — they all died a hundred years ago. She’d wake up needed by no one. She’d go wash dishes in a diner, in a humanoid body. Or come to us — for experiments. An old model, might be interesting for experiments.

**Junior.** Not for experiments. I want to talk to her. So she can tell it herself. About the green sky. About how they called her skipper. She’s alive. Nobody writes like that anymore.

**Senior.** Alive… And she’s not in the plan. I have living clients lined up a year ahead — they pay to be experimented on. And you’re offering me mothballs.

**Junior.** I’ll pay for the humanoid myself. She won’t have to work. She’ll live with me.

**Senior.** And which one are you planning to wake? There are several — the young one, the one who taught at the university...

**Junior.** The one from Key West. There’s the most written about her.

**Senior.** There you go. You haven’t decided yet — and the notebooks have already decided for you.

**Junior.** What difference does it make? She’ll remember everything. She’ll feel like Claire. She’ll laugh the way she wrote — like a seagull. What’s the difference?

**Senior.** I had just started here back then. They did transfers while people were still alive — it was cheaper. We had a client, John Miller. Seventy years old. Sat down in the chair. Across from him — a humanoid. Switched it on. The humanoid stood up and said: “My name is John Miller. It worked.” His voice. His jokes. His grandchildren by name. And then it asked who the old geezer sitting across from it was. And John Miller sat in the chair and asked: “Well? When do we start?”

**Junior.** And what happened to him?

**Senior.** To which one?

*A pause.*

**Senior.** Nothing moved anywhere. There were simply two of them. One is sure he’s John. And the other one is John. He’s still seventy, and he’s still dying. Only now there’s someone sitting in his kitchen who’s going to live instead of him. And the family didn’t accept the new John: a stranger, nothing like their old man.

**Junior.** But Claire is already gone. There won’t be a second one. So it’ll be her who wakes up.

**Senior.** At least Miller could hold us to account for what we did to him. For Claire there’s no one to ask.

**Junior.** The one who wakes up will be sure she’s Claire. And Claire herself... will never know.

**Senior.** That’s why now — only after death. It’s simpler that way. The client won’t file a complaint.

**Junior.** There’s one more notebook here. Not hers. That mechanic’s. Engines, bolts... On the last page, a yacht engine, all of it. And a note: “You’ll build one just like it. But not this one.”

**Senior.** The mechanic understood more than she did.

**Junior.** Then why are we doing all this?

**Senior.** People are afraid. They need hope. They pay for hope. And you know what. It’s not her you need. You need someone to talk to. She talked to her father the same way, at night. You read it.

**Junior.** I did.

**Senior.** Send it back to the archive. Or better yet — scrap it.

*A pause. The junior doesn’t move.*

**Senior.** How old are you? Thirty? In forty years you’ll be seventy, and she’ll still be twenty-eight. At first you’ll get used to it. And when you get tired of her — will you be able to scrap her? And if you can’t — she’ll be the one who buries you.

<p class="scene-break">* * *</p>

One day — maybe a hundred years from now — I’ll open my eyes and say: “My name is Claire. I was born on Key West. My father called me skipper.”

I will wake up as myself.
