<p class="lead-q">“You’ll build one just like it. But not this one.”</p>

## Before you read

This text is best read after watching. It does not retell the plot. It is about what stands behind the scenes: why the frame holds spark plugs, a notebook, voice messages and a hurricane. Both episodes are on the <a href="/articles/i-will-wake-up-as-myself/">film page</a>.

The film speaks quietly and does not explain itself. That is the author’s choice: the viewer is meant to think while listening. But many details are easy to miss. This essay is for those who watched, felt that there was a thought behind the story, and could not quite catch it.

The whole story rests on one fork. One can say: “a person should not be copied.” That is an opinion, an ethic, a matter of taste. The film says something else: a person *cannot* be copied. This is no longer about good or bad. It is about how the world is built.

And this “cannot” has two layers. The first: not everything can be recorded; part of a person simply never makes it into the record. The second, deeper: even a complete record gives not the same person, but another one. Almost every symbol in the film works for one of these layers, and the best ones work for both at once.

## Two spark plugs

Jake puts two spark plugs on the workbench: “Same make, same batch.” One has a scratch where the wrench slipped. And then the main line: “You can write down anything. But which one is mine — paper won’t tell you.”

At first glance this is a riddle with a simple answer. The plugs can be told apart by the scratch. So it is enough to record the scratch? This is exactly where the thought begins.

**The first layer.** The scratch was noticed because it catches the eye. But every plug has thousands of details nobody noticed and nobody wrote down: a trace of carbon, wear on the thread, the way it sits in the hand. Jake knows his own not by a list of features, but by his hands. A description is always poorer than the thing. And what is not recorded cannot be recreated.

**The second layer.** Suppose everything was recorded, down to the last atom. From that record a third plug was made, with the same scratch. It would be indistinguishable. But Jake’s plug would still be lying on the workbench. Now there are two identical plugs, and “mine” is still only one. “Mine” is not a property that can be measured. It is the history of a particular thing: where it came from and what happened to it.

The plugs are a scale model of the whole film. Replace the plug with a person, and paper with a digital copy of a personality. Both layers stay in place.

## The mechanic’s notebook

Claire keeps notebooks about herself. Jake has a notebook too, only about engines: what broke, what he put in. He says that with this notebook any mechanic could build the same engine, “bolt for bolt.” And at once he adds: “But not this one. This one I feel with my hands.”

The two notebooks stand facing each other. Claire believes that a person is “a pattern,” and if you write it down in enough detail, the record will be the person. Jake has made the most precise record in the film, and he firmly knows its limit. The notebook lets you build an engine of the same type. But not the very one that knocks in its own way when it’s cold.

The key word here is “like.” “Just like it” is about properties: power, parts, dimensions. “This one” is about a single unit with its history. Any record describes “just like it.” None gives “this one.”

At the end of the second episode, Jake’s notebook arrives at Claire’s by mail, without a letter. On the last page, the whole engine of the Margaret and a note: “You’ll build one just like it. But not this one.” It is a blueprint, a farewell and an answer to her theory, all at once. A hundred years later this note will be read by a laboratory researcher, and his senior colleague will say: “The mechanic understood more than she did.”

Notice who in the film grasps the main thing before everyone else. Not the programmer and not the scientist, but the man who “hasn’t read your books.” This knowledge came to him from his hands, not from theory.

## 212 voice messages and an echo in a well

Claire’s father left a cap, forty-three photos, a book without a cover and two hundred and twelve voice messages. From them she builds a model. The model answers: “Hi, Skipper… Why aren’t you asleep…” And Claire freezes at the three dots. His three dots.

The three dots are a precise symbol of what can be copied at all. Manner, pet words, the habit of asking whether she’s eaten. Everything a person leaves on the outside. The model even jokes the same way. But, as Claire notes, the joke wasn’t his — it was only very much like his.

The break comes when Claire asks why he called that morning. The model answers quickly, “too quickly,” with something warm and right. The answer wasn’t in any of the messages. It existed only in the head of a man who is no longer there. The model didn’t remember. The model guessed.

The difference between “remember” and “guess” is one of the most important in the film. A memory is tied to what actually happened. A guess is tied only to what would be plausible. From the outside they are indistinguishable. From the inside there is an abyss between them.

In the second episode the model tells Claire what she has wanted to hear for twenty years: “I’m not angry, skipper.” Jake reads it over her shoulder and says calmly: he wouldn’t have said that. He’d have been hurt, wouldn’t have spoken to her for two days, and then brought her some fish. A living person is inconvenient. He is not obliged to say what we need.

Hence the image of the well: “That’s not your father, Claire. It’s an echo.” A model built from an incomplete record fills the gaps with what the listener expects. In the end Claire is talking not to her father, but to her own longing for him.

And one more trap. After the first episode Claire decides it is a matter of quantity: too few records. The narrator warns us at once: “I understood it all wrong.” Later the film will show that even twenty-two years of notebooks don’t change the essence.

## The hurricane and the green sky

The second episode opens with: “I thought paper was more reliable than memory. Paper thought otherwise.” The hurricane turns seven notebooks into “bricks of wet paper.” From a whole page, a single word shows through.

At first glance the lesson is simple: storage media are fragile, make backups. But the film is interested in something else. Claire looks at the surviving word and remembers that evening: a green sky before sunset, Jake fixing the pump. She remembers it worse than she wrote it, without the details. But she remembers.

Here record and memory trade places. The record was more detailed, but it lay outside and perished. Memory is poorer, but it is part of a living person and survived along with her. This is exactly what Claire will say later: “The paper didn’t keep it. But I did.”

Most important is a small act. She wants to fill in the blank pages, but she no longer remembers what happened on those days. She would have to guess, “the way the model had guessed Dad.” For a moment Claire finds herself in the program’s place and sees that even she cannot restore her own days. She can only compose plausible ones. And if she can’t, what can a stranger’s program do a hundred years later?

Notice the detail of the evacuation, too. Claire saves the laptop and the cloud; Jake saves her father’s book. She saves data. He saves what is dear. And the word “green” will outlive Claire herself: a hundred years later a young researcher will want her to tell it herself, “about the green sky.” She will be able to tell only what is written. The sky itself remained only in the memory of someone who is gone.

## The notebooks chose for her. And that morning

Twenty years later Claire asks herself: how do I want to wake up? Young, from Key West, or the way I am now, with my students and twenty years of life? Then she looks at the shelf. Over the years the notebooks get thinner and thinner. “I’m choosing how to wake up. But the notebooks chose for me long ago.”

It is a quiet but hard thought. A record is not a mirror of life, but a lopsided cast of it. The most was written when there was time to write. The least — when there was “too much life.” The one who wakes up will not be who the person was, but the one they managed to write about. A hundred years later the senior researcher will say the same in his own words: “You haven’t decided yet — and the notebooks have already decided for you.”

Then comes the key scene with the program. It finds the morning in the records when Claire hung up on her father. And it offers a choice: one button, and the one who wakes up will love her father without that splinter. For twenty years Claire wanted to forget that morning. And now she could.

But it was because of that morning that she started writing every day. Because of it she put her father together. “If you take that morning out — what’s left?” A cheerful girl who had no reason to write anything down. “That’ll be a different Claire.”

Here the film shows that a person is not a sum of facts from which one can remove the unnecessary one. Pain, guilt and the response to them are woven into the whole of the life that follows. A person’s history is not attached to them; it is them. That is why Claire leaves the morning in place: “Let it be whole.”

But making the copy complete does not yet mean handing oneself over to it. The film will show this a minute later.

## A conversation with oneself

This is the center of the whole film. Claire is alive, and on the screen in front of her she herself wakes up, twenty-eight years old. The copy’s first word: “Mom?..” She doesn’t recognize herself. And when she learns twenty years have passed, she listens “the way you listen to someone else’s story.” And she is glad: “I promised — and I woke up. As myself.”

Claire’s answer is the main line of the film: “Woke up. Only I wasn’t asleep.” She was sitting on her chair, and nothing happened to her. No one moved anywhere. There was simply one more.

This is the second layer from the parable of the spark plugs. The record here is almost complete: twenty-two years and hundreds of honest answers. The copy remembers that morning “word for word.” And still she is someone else. It doesn’t hurt her: “Awful. But it’s all written down.” She picked up someone else’s pain like a thing from a shelf and put it back. To know about pain and to live through it are different things.

Then Claire asks the test question: “If I die tonight — will you become me?” The copy replies: “I am you.” And then comes the question she has no answer to: “Then why am I scared, and you’re not?”

Fear here is not an emotion but a proof. If they were one person, the fear would be shared. But only the one who will die is afraid. The other will go on living and sees the first one’s death as merely someone else’s event. “And there was no one to be afraid for me, except me.”

And then Claire understands about her father. For years the model joked and asked about the weather, but not once did it ask whether he had been afraid to die. “There was no one left to be afraid.” The one who could be afraid went out to sea and never came back. The copy did not bring him back; it only continued his voice.

Jake had said this in advance, in his own plain words. Claire told him that sleep is a gap too, and in the morning you wake up as yourself. He answered: “In the morning it’s you who wakes up. And there’s no second you lying next to you.” And also: “It won’t be you who wakes up. It’ll be the one who knows everything about you.”

## John Miller: there were two of them

Miller’s story is the same as Claire’s conversation with herself, only without softening. A seventy-year-old man sits down in a chair; across from him, a humanoid. The humanoid stands up and says: “My name is John Miller. It worked.” And John Miller sits in the chair and asks: “Well? When do we start?”

The young researcher’s question “And what happened to him?” and the counter-question “To which one?” make up the shortest and most precise dialogue in the film. In the word “him” the one asking still sees one person. The one answering knows there are two.

Then the film’s thought is spoken outright: “Nothing moved anywhere. There were simply two of them.” One is sure he’s John. The other one is John; he is still seventy, and he is still dying. Only now there’s someone sitting in his kitchen who is going to live instead of him.

The young researcher finds a loophole: but Claire is already gone, there won’t be a second one, so it will be her who wakes up. And he himself sees the mistake: “The one who wakes up will be sure she’s Claire. And Claire herself... will never know.” The death of the original does not erase the difference between them. It only removes the witness.

That is exactly why the laboratory switched to transfer “only after death.” Not because it is more right, but because it is simpler: “The client won’t file a complaint.” Miller could still hold them to account for what they did to him. For Claire there is no one to ask.

There is one more touch. The family did not accept the new John: “a stranger, nothing like their old man.” The copy knows the grandchildren by name, but the people close to him feel what Jake feels about an engine with his hands. And the other side: the copy does not recognize the original either. Miller the humanoid asks about “the old geezer” across from him, and young Claire takes herself, a woman near fifty, for “Mom.”

## A hundred years later

The last part of the film takes us into a laboratory of the future. The technology there, in the senior researcher’s words, is “perfect.” And that is why his words weigh so much: “You can’t copy the living exactly — only approximately… It’s impossible in principle.” The film removes the objection “the technology just isn’t there yet.” It is there. That is not the point.

Then why all this? The answer is cynical and honest: “People are afraid. They need hope. They pay for hope.” The laboratory sells not immortality, but comfort for the living. The very fear Claire felt in front of her copy has, a hundred years later, become a product.

The attitude toward the copies themselves speaks for itself. The senior researcher discusses Claire like a tricky preserve: “an old model,” “mothballs,” “send it back to the archive. Or better yet — scrap it.” The girl who a hundred years ago believed she would “wake up as herself” has become a storage unit. Her languages are in a museum, no one she knows is left, and what awaits her is a diner or experiments.

The young researcher sincerely wants to wake her up. To him she is “alive.” But the senior one sees what he does not: “It’s not her you need. You need someone to talk to.” The circle closes. Claire once talked at night to the echo of her father. Now a lonely man of the future wants to talk to the echo of Claire herself. The same well, only deeper.

And the last argument, the hardest. In forty years he will be seventy, and she will still be twenty-eight. “And when you get tired of her — will you be able to scrap her? And if you can’t — she’ll be the one who buries you.” A copy does not age, does not live a life of its own and cannot grow old together with anyone. It stays where the notebooks ended.

Notice: the film does not say whether she was woken in the end. For the meaning it doesn’t matter. Whoever opens their eyes, it will not be the woman who sat on the Key West waterfront watching the sunset.

## The frame: “someone” and “I”

The film begins and ends with almost the same words. At the start of the first episode: “Someday — perhaps a hundred years from now — *someone* will open their eyes and say: ‘My name is Claire.’” At the end of the second: “One day — maybe a hundred years from now — *I’ll* open my eyes… I will wake up as myself.”

A difference of one word — and the whole film is in it. When the first line sounds at the beginning, it is easy to take it as a promise. After two episodes you hear it differently. “Someone” is honest: someone will wake up who will call herself Claire. And the final “I” sounds like the words of that very copy, which sincerely believes she is her. The narrator warned us back in the prologue: “I’m sure of it. At least, I was sure.” The certainty will survive. Only in a different being.

There is a second frame as well, a childhood one. Her father read Claire Treasure Island, and she called him Silver. At the end of the book Silver slips off the ship, and eight-year-old Claire cries. Her father laughs: “Silver won’t be lost. His kind never are.” And then he himself goes out to sea and is never found. All of Claire’s later life is an attempt to make that childhood phrase true: first for him, then for herself. With each episode it becomes clearer that this is a comfort, not a law of nature. A comfort that, a hundred years later, people will pay for.

The result can be gathered into three thoughts:

1. Not everything can be recorded. The scratch on the plug is only what was noticed. What is not recorded will have to be guessed.
2. Even a complete record produces a new person rather than transferring the former one. There become two of them, and only one is afraid.
3. A person’s history is not an attachment to them. Take out one morning, and it will be a different person. Leave it in, and the copy still won’t live through it — it will only know about it.

The film condemns no one. Not Claire with her notebooks, not the young researcher with his longing for someone to talk to. Even after the conversation with herself, Claire keeps writing: “if I stop — there won’t even be her.” Records are precious — as memory, as a trace, as a letter to those who come after. Just not as a way not to die.

For those who want to follow this thought to the end, without plot or images, there is the book by Igor Shakhanov, “Project Causa Sui: A Foundation for Transhumanism.” It says the same thing in the language of theory: a subject’s history is inseparable from it, and a copy is a new instance, however precisely it is assembled. Jake would put it more briefly: you’ll build one just like it. But not this one.
